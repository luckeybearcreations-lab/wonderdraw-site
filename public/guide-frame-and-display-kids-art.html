How to frame and display kids’ art at home

A drawing on the fridge curls, gets splashed and disappears under the next one. A little framing turns the same page into something the whole family notices. None of it has to be expensive or permanent.

Pick the pieces worth a frame

Not every drawing needs glass. Choose a handful: one your child is proud of, one with a story, one that simply makes you smile. Ask your child which piece they want on the wall. Their pick may not be yours, and it is their gallery too.

Measure the paper first

Kids’ art comes in awkward sizes. Printer paper is 8.5 by 11 inches, construction paper is often 9 by 12, and easel paper is larger still. Measure the actual page before you buy anything, because school paper is not always cut to a standard size.

A frame one size up with a mat is more forgiving than an exact fit. A letter-size drawing sits well in an 11 by 14 inch frame with a mat cut for it.

Use a mat

A mat is the card border between the art and the frame. It hides ragged edges, gives a busy drawing some breathing room and keeps the surface from pressing against the glass. That last part matters for crayon, oil pastel and thick paint, which can stick or smear.

If you want the piece to last for years, look for mats and backing boards labeled acid-free.

Do not trim or glue the original

Resist cutting a drawing down to fit a frame. Hold it in place with photo corners, or with a small piece of removable tape along the top edge on the back. Before it goes in, pencil your child’s name, the date and what the picture shows on the back.

Try a frame that opens from the front

Some frames are made for kids’ art: the front swings open like a door, so swapping in a new piece takes a few seconds, and many hold a stack of older drawings behind the one on show. They suit a child who brings home something new every week.

Displays that need no frame

A line and clips. Run a string or wire along a wall and hang drawings with clothespins or small clips.

Clipboards. A row of clipboards on small hooks makes a tidy grid that a child can change without help.

A cork or magnet board. One board with a clear edge keeps the display from creeping across the wall.

Paper tape. Decorative paper tape around a drawing makes a quick frame. Test it on a hidden patch of paint first.

Choose the spot with care

Keep art out of direct sun. Marker and construction paper fade quickly in bright light. Steam and splashes are hard on paper too, so a hallway, a stairway or a bedroom wall usually works better than right beside the stove or in a bathroom.

Hang at least some pieces at your child’s eye level. In a child’s room, frames with an acrylic front are a safer choice than glass, and heavy frames are better kept away from the wall above a bed.

Build a gallery wall that can grow

Frames in one color and several sizes look planned even when the art is wildly different. Lay the arrangement out on the floor first, or tape paper cutouts of each frame to the wall and move them around until it looks right. Keep the gaps between frames even, and leave room at one side to add more.

Rotate, and date what comes down

A display stays interesting when it changes. Swap pieces every few weeks or at the start of each season, and store the retired ones flat. Our guide on what to do with the pile of kids’ artwork covers sorting and storing what comes off the wall.

When the original is fragile or bulky

Glitter, glued-on pasta and thick collage do not sit well behind glass. A deep shadow-box frame handles those. For chalk or pastel that smudges, or a painting too big for any wall you have, frame a printed photo instead and keep the original stored flat.
